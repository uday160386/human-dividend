Method

About {{ site.title }}

Most writing about AI comes from the companies selling it, the consultants advising on it, or people worried about it. {{ site.title }} keeps a plain record of something narrower: documented cases where an ordinary person was better off. Every case comes from journalism, peer‑reviewed research, a university, a public body or a non‑profit.

What counts as a source

Journalism (NBC, TODAY, The Guardian, Stateline), journals (NEJM, Nature, The Lancet, QJE), universities, the NHS and UK government, the World Bank, and non‑profits.

What was left out

Vendor case studies, AI‑lab blog posts, consulting reports, and social‑media testimonials, even when they told a good story.

Who counts as "common people"

Patients, parents, students, farmers, frontline workers and people with disabilities. Not executives, not investors.

The fine print

  • These are success stories. AI also makes confident mistakes, and the people above did best when a human expert, a teacher or a doctor stayed in the loop.
  • Several of the strongest results come from clinical trials with implants or hospital systems, not from apps anyone can download today.
  • In the call‑centre study, the most experienced workers saw small drops in quality. Gains were real but uneven.
  • Some figures are early analyses or single cases. Where a randomised trial exists (Sweden, Nigeria, the call centres), it is noted.
